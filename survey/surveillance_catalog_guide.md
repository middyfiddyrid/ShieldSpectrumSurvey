## Drone / RC surveillance UAS — control uplink + video downlink RF signatures (2.4 GHz & 5.8 GHz FHSS/OFDM, 900 MHz & 1.2/1.3 GHz long-range, plus WiFi drones and DroneID/RemoteID)

A surveillance drone almost never emits a single signal — it emits a control-link signature (uplink, narrowband FHSS/LoRa, sub-GHz or 2.4 GHz) AND a video-downlink signature (wideband, 5.8 GHz or 2.4 GHz analog/OFDM), and often a third RemoteID/DroneID beacon. These are DISTINCT on the air and each gets its own row: the control link is a low-bandwidth hopper you find by its hop pattern and duty; the video link is a fat continuous carrier (analog FM) or a ~10-40 MHz OFDM block (DJI OcuSync/Lightbridge, Walksnail Avatar, HDZero). Separated by BAND (900/1200/1300/2400/5200/5800 MHz), CHANNEL WIDTH, MODULATION (analog FM video vs OFDM digital vs LoRa vs GFSK/FHSS vs Wi-Fi), and protocol variant where the on-air picture genuinely differs. Consumer camera drones (DJI Mavic/Air/Mini, Autel) collapse control+video into one proprietary OcuSync/SkyLink OFDM+FHSS stack; FPV/custom builds split them across separate radios (ELRS/Crossfire control + analog or Walksnail/HDZero video). CRITICAL REACH: the two headline surveillance bands, 5.725-5.925 GHz (video) and 5.2 GHz, are ABOVE a stock ADALM-Pluto's 3800 MHz ceiling (stock Pluto = AD9363, 325-3800 MHz) — they need a B210 (70 MHz-6 GHz) or an AD9364-modded/re-tuned Pluto with an external 5.8 GHz front end. A stock Pluto only reaches the 900 MHz, 1.2/1.3 GHz, and 2.4 GHz signatures. Nothing here is above 6 GHz, so a B210 covers the entire class. Massive legitimate-device overlap exists at 2.4 GHz (Wi-Fi/BLE) and 900 MHz (ISM telemetry, LoRa IoT, cordless), so band presence alone is never a detection — the hop signature, duty, and video-carrier shape are what identify a drone.

**How to confirm:**
- Look for the SIGNATURE PAIR, not a single signal: a wideband video slab/carrier (analog fat FM or ~20-40 MHz OFDM) co-present with a narrow hopping control skirt in the same or a paired band. A lone fat carrier could be many things; the pair is the drone tell.
- On the waterfall, check DUTY to split control from video: control links hop in short narrow bursts on a regular cadence (ELRS ~50-1000 Hz on 2.4 / up to 200 Hz on 900 is metronome-regular; FrSky/Crossfire have their own cadence); video is a steady continuous slab. RemoteID/DroneID is burst-then-silent (~600 ms period).
- Distinguish analog vs digital video: analog FM shows a peaked center carrier with FM video sidebands and audio subcarrier; digital (DJI/Walksnail/HDZero) shows a flat-topped noise-like OFDM block with sharp band edges. Width helps: DJI 20/40 MHz, HDZero ~27 MHz (17 MHz narrow), analog ~17-18 MHz occupied.
- For 5.8 GHz and 5.2 GHz you MUST use a B210 or a modded/downconverted Pluto — a stock Pluto (AD9363, 325-3800 MHz) simply cannot see these bands; confirm your front end reaches 5.9 GHz before concluding 'no drone.'
- Try to decode DJI DroneID / Broadcast RemoteID (open-source demods exist — proto17/dji_droneid for the 2.4 GHz OFDM burst and for BLE/Wi-Fi-NAN ASTM F3411): a successful decode yields drone serial + operator GPS/home point — definitive identification and operator localization.
- Correlate with motion: point a directional antenna and watch RSSI track a moving airborne source. A benign Wi-Fi AP or IoT sensor is stationary; a drone's downlink RSSI and Doppler shift move. This is the key discriminator against the huge 2.4 GHz/900 MHz false-positive pool.
- Sweep the sub-GHz long-range bands (433, 900/915, 1.2-1.3 GHz) explicitly — covert long-range platforms deliberately avoid crowded 2.4/5.8 GHz. Don't stop at 2.4/5.8.
- Time-correlate control and video: arming/flight begins when a continuous video slab appears alongside an active control hopper; both go quiet together on landing/disarm. Simultaneous on/off across two bands is strong confirmation.

**Might legitimately be:**
- 2.4 GHz digital video and Wi-Fi-drone signatures are visually IDENTICAL to ordinary 802.11 Wi-Fi (routers, laptops, phones, cameras, IoT) and to Bluetooth/BLE — this band is the single biggest false-positive minefield. Band presence alone is never a detection.
- 5.8/5.2 GHz digital video overlaps 5 GHz Wi-Fi (802.11a/n/ac/ax APs, wireless HDMI, some baby monitors) and 5 GHz cordless — a 20/40 MHz OFDM slab here is often just a home/office AP.
- 900/915 MHz control and telemetry overlap LoRaWAN/IoT, LPWAN, 900 MHz cordless phones, some smart meters, ISM sensors, and cellular guard bands — ELRS/Crossfire/SiK hoppers look like generic 900 MHz ISM traffic.
- 433 MHz telemetry overlaps LPD433 remotes, ham 70 cm, car key fobs, TPMS, weather stations, garage doors — extremely crowded, low-confidence band on its own.
- 1.2-1.3 GHz analog video overlaps GPS L2 (1227 MHz), amateur 23 cm, ATC transponders/DME, and radiosondes — a legit FM-video-shaped carrier is the tell but adjacent services cause confusion.
- ELRS/FrSky/Spektrum control-link FHSS/DSSS hops resemble Bluetooth classic hopping and other 2.4 GHz FHSS ISM devices (some wireless mics, industrial telemetry).
- Broadcast RemoteID over BLE/Wi-Fi is, by design, indistinguishable at the RF layer from any other BLE beacon or Wi-Fi frame — only payload decode confirms it's a drone.
- DJI/Autel OFDM downlinks resemble each other and resemble some proprietary wireless-video/streaming gear — cross-vendor shape overlap means timing/RemoteID decode is needed to attribute.

**TSCM note:** Threat framing for a bug sweep: a surveillance drone is a MOBILE, INTERMITTENT emitter — it is only on the air while flying, so a one-shot spectrum snapshot will miss it. Sweep continuously and, ideally, during the windows when overflight is suspected. The highest-value TSCM win here is decoding DJI DroneID or Broadcast RemoteID: those beacons transmit the operator's GPS position and the drone's home point IN THE CLEAR (DJI DroneID is unencrypted and reverse-engineered — proto17/dji_droneid, NDSS 2023), so a successful decode can localize the person flying it — flag this capability to the client. Practical detection reality: because 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz are saturated with Wi-Fi/BLE and 900 MHz with IoT, you cannot call a drone from band presence alone — you need the control+video signature PAIR, the moving-source RSSI/Doppler behavior, or a payload decode. Prioritize a purpose-built drone-detection front end or the B210 for 5.x GHz; a stock ADALM-Pluto (AD9363, 325-3800 MHz) blindly misses the two most common video bands (5.2/5.8 GHz) and will give a false 'all clear' if it is the only receiver. Long-range/covert operators deliberately use 433/900 MHz/1.2 GHz to stay off the crowded bands defenders watch, so include the sub-GHz sweep. Note legal/exposure angle for the client: intercepting/decoding another party's drone control or video may implicate wiretap/interception statutes and FAA rules on interfering with aircraft — passive detection and RemoteID reception are generally fine, but active jamming/takeover is federally prohibited and should be surfaced to counsel before any 'counter-drone' action is proposed.


## Covert body-worn transmitters & miscellaneous covert data links

This class covers the "wire" a person carries and adjacent low-profile covert data links, as distinct from fixed room bugs. On the air these break into four families: (1) classic crystal/PLL narrowband-FM analog body wires spread across the traditional surveillance segments - low-VHF ~30-50 MHz, 88-108 MHz (parasitic on FM broadcast), 108-136 MHz aircraft band, the "hot" 135-174 MHz law-enforcement/wireless-mic segment, 174-216 MHz, the very common 315/433/868 MHz ISM sub-GHz band, and UHF 380-512 MHz; (2) narrowband SSB/AM covert links (rarer, favored for range/low power); (3) digital/encrypted body wires in the 902-928 MHz ISM and 2.400-2.4835 GHz ISM bands, typically FHSS or narrow digital carriers with AES; and (4) burst / store-and-forward data bugs that digitize, compress, and dump audio in short bursts, including GSM/LTE cellular exfiltration. The key TSCM insight is that band + channel width + modulation + duty is the signature, not the make. A stock PlutoSDR (325-3800 MHz) reaches almost everything from 325 MHz up (including the extremely common 433/868 MHz ISM bugs) EXCEPT low-VHF (30-50 MHz) and the 88-108/108-136/135-325 MHz range, which need the AD9364 frequency-extension mod or a downconverter/upconverter; the B210 (70 MHz-6 GHz) covers all of these natively (except below 70 MHz). The hardest targets are the intermittent SSB wires and the burst/store-and-forward devices, which are silent 99%+ of the time and demand long unattended spectrum logging rather than a walk-through sweep. Major honest caveat: nearly every band here is shared with legitimate LMR, wireless mics, IoT, cordless devices, remote controls, and cellular - analysis is about isolating an unexplained emitter near the protectee, not about a modulation being inherently hostile.

**How to confirm:**
- Demodulate every suspect narrowband VHF/UHF/ISM carrier as FM (and also as USB/LSB for the SSB case) and listen: recovering live room/body audio, or hearing your own test tone/rustle correlated with the protectee's movement, confirms a voice wire. A carrier with LMR trunking control, CTCSS/DCS-only idle, or a short periodic data protocol (keyfob/TPMS/telemetry) is NOT a covert wire.
- Localize with a near-field probe / directional antenna and RSSI: walk toward the suspected wearer or fixture - a covert wire's signal rises sharply within a meter or two. A signal that does NOT localize to the protected person/space is almost certainly a licensed background emitter.
- Cross-check the frequency against licensed LMR/FCC ULS and known site systems: an FM/P25 voice carrier on a channel with no license on record and no operational reason to be at this location is the flag.
- For 315/433/868 MHz suspects specifically, discriminate on DUTY: legitimate ISM devices (remotes, TPMS, sensors) transmit brief periodic bursts, while an audio wire holds a continuous voice-bearing carrier - a persistent carrier on 433.92 MHz near the protectee is a strong flag.
- For 900 MHz / 2.4 GHz / 5.8 GHz digital suspects: capture IQ and inventory every legitimate device (Wi-Fi APs, BT/BLE, Zigbee, wireless mics, cordless). Anything left over after accounting for known devices - especially a carrier that tracks a person's movement - warrants teardown/physical search.
- For FHSS suspects, use max-hold / persistence over minutes to reveal the hop comb across the full ISM band, then estimate hop rate and dwell; compare against known BT/BLE and cordless patterns to isolate an unexplained hopper.
- For burst / store-and-forward and scheduled cellular exfil: run UNATTENDED wideband logging for 24-72 h with event capture; look for an isolated digital burst that repeats on a fixed schedule (e.g. every N hours) with no human activity behind it.
- For GSM/LTE 'bug' suspects: use near-field cellular detection - identify a transmitting cellular emitter co-located with the protectee/room that has no legitimate phone/hotspot explanation; corroborate with a physical search of AC units, power strips, chargers, and outlets.
- Confirm any find physically: RF localization narrows the location, but a TSCM confirmation is the recovered device in hand, not a spectral peak. Photograph, do not power-cycle, and preserve for chain of custody.

**Might legitimately be:**
- 150-174 MHz and 380-512 MHz narrowband FM voice is overwhelmingly legitimate business/public-safety LMR - a covert wire looks identical in modulation; only lack of signaling + localization + no-license separates them.
- 88-108 MHz carriers are almost all licensed FM broadcast; parasitic wires hide precisely by resembling them.
- 315/433/868 MHz ISM audio wires share the band with a huge population of keyfobs, TPMS sensors, weather stations, garage/gate remotes, and low-power telemetry - the discriminator is a continuous voice-bearing carrier vs. the brief periodic bursts of legit ISM devices.

- 2.4 GHz digital and FHSS body wires are drowned in Wi-Fi, Bluetooth/BLE, Zigbee, RC, baby monitors, and video links - the most false-positive-prone band on the list.
- 5.8 GHz covert A/V overlaps 5 GHz Wi-Fi and FPV drone video.
- GSM/LTE covert links are indistinguishable on spectrum from any nearby legitimate phone, hotspot, tablet, or wearable - near-field localization is the ONLY separator.

- Burst/store-and-forward blocks can resemble routine scheduled IoT check-ins, meter reads, or telemetry beacons.

**TSCM note:** Prioritize by how the target hides in TIME and BAND, not by exotic modulation. The continuous analog FM wires (VHF/UHF and the very common 315/433/868 MHz ISM bugs) are the easiest catch and should fall out of any competent walk-through sweep - the work there is discrimination (is this carrier a licensed LMR channel, a legit ISM burst, or an unexplained continuous wire near my protectee?), which means FM/SSB demod + localization + duty analysis + a license cross-check, never a bare peak. The genuinely hard members of this class are the ones that are silent almost all the time: SSB voice-operated wires (narrow, only present on speech), and above all the burst / store-and-forward and scheduled cellular-exfil bugs. Those defeat a one-pass sweep by design and require unattended 24-72 h wideband logging with scheduled-burst anomaly detection - build that logging mode into the app, because a real-time matcher alone will miss them. For every ISM-band digital suspect, the correct workflow is inventory-and-subtract: enumerate all legitimate emitters, then investigate the residue, especially any emitter whose RSSI tracks a person. Encryption (AES on P25/DMR and the 900 MHz/2.4 GHz digital wires) means you will confirm the SIGNATURE, never the content - set expectations accordingly. Reach: a stock Pluto (325-3800 MHz) misses the entire low-VHF/FM-broadcast/108-325 MHz swath where a large share of classic body wires live (and just misses 315 MHz), but DOES cover the extremely common 433/868 MHz ISM bugs - either add the AD9364 frequency-extension mod, pair a cheap RTL-SDR for a 24-1700 MHz first look, or use the B210 (70 MHz-6 GHz) which covers everything here except below 70 MHz and 5G mmWave. Above 6 GHz is out of B210 entirely (not a concern for this class except mmWave 5G exfil). Finally, per TSCM firms, GSM/LTE bugs are cited as near-impossible without proper near-field cellular gear - flag to the client that a stock SDR sweep is necessary but not sufficient for cellular exfil, and recommend a dedicated near-field cellular detector as a companion tool.


## Cellular GPS vehicle/asset trackers (magnetic, OBD-II, hardwired, battery-covert) — detected on the cellular UPLINK only, not the GPS side

Cellular GPS trackers are the single most common covert TSCM find on vehicles, yet the GPS/GNSS receiver inside them EMITS NOTHING — it only listens to satellites. The ONLY detectable RF signature is the cellular UPLINK the device uses to phone location home: a 2G/3G/4G-LTE/LTE-M/NB-IoT/5G transmission on a standard carrier uplink band. The defining detection problem is DUTY CYCLE: most modern trackers are interval reporters (PSM/eDRX low-power modes) that sleep silent for minutes to hours (or a full day), wake for a few hundred ms to a few seconds, GNSS-fix, attach, dump a location packet, and drop back to sleep. Between reports the device is RF-invisible — a single spectrum sweep will almost always miss it. Effective detection requires DWELL: park the vehicle, leave a wideband monitor watching the LTE/NB-IoT uplink bands for a full report interval (30+ min, ideally hours), and catch the periodic burst. A moving vehicle in real-time (10-60 s update) mode is far easier to catch because it transmits almost continuously. The signatures below are organized by radio-access technology and uplink band because that is what the SDR actually sees; the thousands of tracker models collapse into this handful of on-air signatures. REACH NOTE: every uplink band in this class sits inside a stock ADALM-Pluto's 325-3800 MHz range EXCEPT 5G NR mid-band n77/n78 at 3.3-3.8 GHz, which is at or just past the Pluto's 3800 MHz upper edge. There is no sub-325 MHz gap — the lowest band here is 600 MHz. A B210 (70 MHz-6 GHz) covers everything in this class, including n78.

**How to confirm:**
- Understand the core trap first: the GPS receiver emits nothing. You are hunting the CELLULAR UPLINK, and most trackers sleep between reports. A one-pass sweep proves nothing — absence of signal is NOT absence of device.
- DWELL, don't sweep. Park the vehicle in a quiet RF spot, set the SDR to watch the candidate uplink band(s) with a waterfall, and leave it running for at least one full report interval — 30-60 min minimum, ideally several hours or overnight — to catch a periodic burst. Log timestamps of any repeating bursts; a fixed inter-burst interval (e.g., every 60 s, 5 min, 60 min, 24 h) is the tracker tell.
- Force the device to talk: drive the vehicle. Motion often triggers real-time/frequent reporting on motion-activated trackers, converting a silent interval reporter into a near-continuous uplink you can catch. Watch the LTE low-band (600/700/850) and AWS/PCS uplinks while moving.
- Provoke a report: some units report on ignition-on, geofence-cross, or a shock/tow event. Cycle ignition, move the vehicle a few hundred meters, or bump it, and watch for a correlated uplink burst.
- Correlate direction with a directional antenna / near-field probe. Once you see a repeating uplink burst, use a directional log-periodic or a near-field sniffer to walk the signal to its source location on the vehicle (wheel wells, bumpers, under seats, OBD-II port, behind trim, inside battery/fuse box).
- Physically inspect the highest-probability mounts regardless of RF: OBD-II port (plug-in trackers report here and are trivial to find), under-hood/battery for hardwired units, steel surfaces for magnetic units (frame rails, wheel wells, inside bumpers, tow hitch, under spare tire).
- Distinguish tech on the waterfall: 200 kHz constant-envelope GMSK TDMA burst = 2G; ~5 MHz noise-like = 3G WCDMA; SC-FDMA variable-RB burst = LTE Cat-1; ~1.4 MHz short burst = LTE-M; a single ~4 kHz pip or <180 kHz burst on a fixed raster = NB-IoT.
- Rule out the phone/host vehicle: a modern car has its OWN embedded cellular modem (telematics/eCall) and the occupant's phone, both of which transmit on these exact bands. Confirm the burst source is NOT the head unit/TCU or a phone before declaring a covert tracker — geolocate the emitter physically.
- For NB-IoT/single-tone suspects, use maximum FFT resolution (narrow RBW) and the longest dwell you can afford — these devices may transmit for only seconds per day.
- If the suspected band is 5G n78 (3.5 GHz) or you need >3.8 GHz headroom, switch from a stock Pluto to a B210 (or equivalent) to stay in range and get enough instantaneous bandwidth.

**Might legitimately be:**
- The occupant's own smartphone transmits on the IDENTICAL LTE/5G uplink bands — this is the #1 false positive. Any cellular uplink burst could be a phone in the vehicle.
- The vehicle's own embedded telematics/eCall modem (GM OnStar, Ford, Toyota, BMW, etc.) is a factory-installed cellular tracker on these same bands — legitimate but indistinguishable on the air from a covert unit without physical location.
- Legitimate fleet/insurance/teen-driver/usage-based-insurance (UBI) trackers and OBD-II dongles use exactly these signatures and are often installed with the owner's consent — presence of a cellular tracker is not proof of illicit surveillance.
- Nearby vehicles, pedestrians, and buildings all radiate the same uplink bands — in any populated area the LTE uplink spectrum is crowded, so a random burst is meaningless without repetition + physical geolocation.
- IoT devices unrelated to tracking (smart tolls, connected sensors, cellular alarm panels, e-readers, some smartwatches) also use LTE-M/NB-IoT bursts.
- 2G GMSK bursts in-band can be from femtocells, IMSI-catchers, or test equipment rather than a tracker.
- In the US, a live 2G/3G uplink cannot connect to a shut-down macro network, so such a signal is more likely lab gear, a private base station, or a jammer than a functioning tracker — do not over-index on it as a tracker find.

**TSCM note:** Operationally, cellular GPS trackers are the highest-yield vehicle TSCM find and the easiest to physically discover (OBD-II and magnetic units especially), yet the WORST to find by RF sweep because of duty cycle. Do not rely on the spectrum analyzer alone: a thorough physical search of the OBD-II port, battery/fuse box, wheel wells, bumpers, undercarriage, and cabin (steel surfaces for magnets) will find more trackers than any single RF pass. Use RF to CONFIRM and geolocate an emitter and to catch battery units with no obvious mount. The decisive technique is a long dwell on the carrier uplink bands (a spectrum-monitoring recording left running through at least one report interval, better overnight) plus motion/ignition provocation to force a report. Interval/PSM/eDRX devices can be silent for a full day; the honest client message is that a clean single sweep does NOT clear a vehicle, and a combined physical + long-dwell RF search over time is required. There is no sub-325 MHz gap in this class (lowest band is 600 MHz).


## Cellular "SIM" audio bugs (call-to-listen / auto-dial covert GSM/LTE/5G eavesdropping devices)

A cellular SIM bug is a stripped-down cellphone/modem (transmitter + mic + SIM, often a battery) hidden in an everyday object. The operator calls the bug's number (call-to-listen) or the bug auto-dials/streams on VOX (auto-dial), and the room audio rides the mobile network — unlimited range. On the air, a SIM bug IS a phone: it emits exactly the standard cellular UPLINK signature for whatever radio it carries. So the signatures below are just the cellular uplink emissions the SDR can see, one row per genuinely distinct band x channel-width x modulation x duty combination. There is NO magic "bug waveform" — the entire TSCM problem is duty/behavior/context, not modulation. Two facts dominate reach: (1) a stock PlutoSDR tops out at 3800 MHz (325-3800 stock; the AD9364 firmware/clock mod extends ~70 MHz-6 GHz, unguaranteed/uncalibrated at the edges), so most low/mid cellular UPLINK (GSM 850/900, LTE/5G bands below 2.7 GHz uplink) is in reach, but n41 (2496-2690 TDD) and 5G n77/n78 (3.3-3.8/3.3-4.2 GHz) sit at or past the stock edge; (2) a B210 covers 70 MHz-6 GHz so it reaches all sub-6 cellular uplink but NOTHING mmWave (5G FR2 24-40 GHz) is reachable by either radio. - Extend the scan window to at least 2525 MHz (not just the 2400-2483.5 Wi-Fi ISM band) so nRF24 channels 84-125 above the ISM edge are actually in view — well inside stock Pluto/B210 range.
- Dwell long, or provoke: type on / move the suspect keyboard/mouse and watch for correlated <1 ms GFSK bursts. Idle nRF24 radios are silent, so a short sweep with no HID activity will show nothing.
- Measure occupied bandwidth to split rates: ~1 MHz => 250 kbps or 1 Mbps; ~2 MHz => 2 Mbps. Then demod symbol rate to separate 250 kbps from 1 Mbps.
- Demodulate/decode to confirm Enhanced ShockBurst framing (preamble + address + CRC). Presence in-band is NOT enough — GFSK bursts alias against BLE, Zigbee, and other ISM sources.
- For injection tools (CrazyRadio/MouseJack): look for elevated-power GFSK bursts sweeping across many 1 MHz channels in rapid succession near a workstation, not a single fixed-channel link.
- For KeySweeper-class loggers: the logger may be radio-silent on 2.4 GHz — hunt the separate exfil path (GSM 850/1900, Wi-Fi, LoRa) and physically inspect USB chargers/wall warts and anything plugged near the keyboard.
- Treat unencrypted/plaintext keystroke traffic (KeySniffer MOSART/Signia class) as high-severity — decode a burst to check whether keystrokes are in the clear.
- Set the Pluto to sweep 2400-2485 MHz (extend to 2525 to catch high nRF24 channels) with fast frame rate; a resting wireless keyboard shows near-nothing, so you must generate events.
- Have the client type on / move each suspect peripheral and correlate: real HID bursts appear ONLY when the user acts. Bursts that appear with no user action = injection or a second radio -> escalate.
- On the waterfall, classify the burst: clean two-lobe = GFSK (nRF24/MOSART/MS); flat noise-like spread = Cypress DSSS; hopping across many channels per event = Logitech Unifying FHSS; single fixed channel = MOSART/MS.
- Enumerate USB receiver vendor/product IDs on the client machine (Logitech, MOSART 062A, Microsoft, Cypress) to map dongle to link type, since the on-air GFSK burst is otherwise ambiguous.
- For a suspected KeySweeper-class logger: do NOT rely on the HID band. Physically inspect wall chargers, power strips, and USB pass-throughs, AND sweep for an out-of-band exfil uplink (GSM/LTE 700-1900, Wi-Fi 2.4/5, LoRa 915) that switches on around typing.
- Confirm encryption exposure: identify any Microsoft pre-2011 XOR or MOSART unencrypted keyboard in the sensitive space and recommend swapping to a Bolt/BLE-AES or wired keyboard.
- Use a dedicated nRF24 tool (Crazyradio + MouseJack/LOGITacker) only to positively fingerprint a link if the Pluto sweep is inconclusive; note this is beyond a stock Pluto's demod ability.
- Zoom the SDR span across 2400-2525 MHz and look for narrow (~2 MHz) GFSK bursts; measure occupied bandwidth - ~2 MHz indicates the 2 Mbps variant, ~1 MHz indicates the standard 1 Mbps row.
- Confirm bursty duty: capture IQ and verify short packetized on-air bursts with idle/ACK gaps (Enhanced ShockBurst), not continuous carrier and not wideband OFDM.
- Watch for channel agility - note whether the burst stays on one 1 MHz-center channel or hops; log the exact center frequency.
- Cross-check against known 2.4 GHz occupants: rule out Wi-Fi (20/40 MHz OFDM), BLE (1 MHz FHSS advertising on 2402/2426/2480), and classic BT (1600 hops/s) by bandwidth and hop pattern.
- If keystroke-injection risk is suspected, corroborate with a dedicated nRF24 tool (Crazyradio PA + mousejack/scanner) to demodulate ShockBurst addresses - the SDR shows the signature; the nRF24 sniffer confirms protocol and device.
- Correlate bursts with the target's use of a wireless mouse/keyboard or an unexplained USB nano-receiver during the sweep.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Legitimate wireless mice, keyboards, trackpads, and presentation clickers — these ARE nRF24/MOSART/Microsoft-2.4G devices and produce byte-for-byte the same PHY signature as a covert logger. Presence of the signature is expected in almost any office; it is NOT by itself evidence of surveillance.
- Wi-Fi (2412-2472 MHz, 20/40/80 MHz OFDM) overlaps the band but is far wider and flat-topped — easy to distinguish by bandwidth, but a narrow nRF24 burst can hide UNDER an active Wi-Fi channel and be masked on a low-resolution display.
- Bluetooth / BLE — Bluetooth classic hops FHSS across 2402-2480 at 1 MHz spacing with 1 Mbps GFSK, which is genuinely close to nRF24 1 Mbps. Distinguish by the fast, structured hop pattern (BT) versus fixed-channel or slow-agility (nRF24), and by BLE's 2 MHz-spaced advertising channels (2402/2426/2480).
- Zigbee / 802.15.4 (2405-2480, 2 MHz-wide O-QPSK) — similar burstiness and channel raster; distinguish by modulation shape (O-QPSK vs GFSK) and Zigbee's 5 MHz channel spacing.
- Wireless game controllers, RF barcode scanners, wireless headsets/mics on proprietary 2.4G, drone/RC links (some use nRF24), and countless ISM 'toys' (weather stations that stray up, wireless doorbells, etc.) — many share the exact GFSK burst profile.
- Microwave-oven leakage and other wideband ISM noise can raise the noise floor across the band and mask or mimic activity, causing false alarms on energy-only detectors.
- BLE advertising/data (GFSK 1 Mbps/2 Mbps, ~1-2 MHz, bursty) — nearly identical spectral footprint; separate only by protocol decode
- 802.15.4 / Zigbee (OQPSK 2 MHz channels, periodic) — overlaps the 2 Mbps width
- Legitimate wireless keyboards/mice you are not investigating (same silicon)
- Wi-Fi 20 MHz bursts (much wider, easy to exclude by width)
- RC/drone control links and hobby telemetry on nRF24-family radios
- Microwave-oven leakage and other ISM noise (broadband, non-framed — excluded by demod)
- MoCA/other spurs occasionally seen just above 2483.5 MHz
- BLE advertising / connection bursts (1-2 MHz GFSK, same band, constant background in any modern space)
- Wi-Fi 2.4 GHz OFDM (wide, but its energy floods the same channels and masks HID bursts)
- Zigbee / Thread / 802.15.4 (2 MHz O-QPSK bursts on 2405-2480, look burst-like)
- Legitimate wireless mice/keyboards/presenters — indistinguishable on RF from a KeySweeper logger; only user-action correlation separates them
- Microwave oven leakage and other ISM noise near 2450 MHz
- Bluetooth Classic frequency hopping (also hops the band, can be confused with Unifying FHSS)
- RC toys/drones on 2.4 GHz GFSK (also nRF24-based in many cases)
- Legitimate Logitech Unifying wireless mice and keyboards (same nRF24 link, 2 Mbps)
- Generic 2.4 GHz non-Bluetooth wireless keyboards/mice with USB nano-receivers
- Wireless presentation clickers/remotes
- RC toys, drones (Crazyflie), and game controllers on nRF24
- Industrial/hobby sensor telemetry links using nRF24L01+ at 2 Mbps
- General 2.4 GHz ISM congestion (Wi-Fi/BLE/BT edges) can mask or be confused with short nRF24 bursts until bandwidth and duty are measured

**TSCM note:** Why this class matters and how to treat it: the covert-exfil risk is that a hardware keystroke logger or data bug using an nRF24/MOSART/Microsoft-2.4G link is SPECTRALLY INDISTINGUISHABLE from the wireless mouse dongle sitting next to it. You cannot flag this class on signature alone — every finding must be resolved by inventory subtraction (turn off all known peripherals) and behavioral correlation (does it track keystrokes? does it heartbeat when idle? does it have a receiver ACKing it?). The highest-signal covert tells are: (1) a steady idle keep-alive/heartbeat with no attributable peripheral, (2) a link that does not correlate with any known input device, and (3) presence of an auto-ACK reply proving a hidden receiver in range. Operationally: many of these links (MOSART/KeySniffer, older Microsoft, un-patched Logitech Unifying/MouseJack) are UNENCRYPTED or keystroke-injectable, so the same sweep that finds them can, with an nRF24 receiver, capture cleartext to prove exfil — a strong client deliverable, but only under proper authorization. Do NOT auto-classify a 2.4 GHz GFSK burst as a bug in a client report; describe it as 'proprietary 2.4 GHz peripheral-class link, attribution pending physical inventory,' and escalate only the un-attributable ones. Plain-language label for client-facing output: 'wireless keyboard/mouse-style 2.4 GHz link (could be a normal wireless mouse or a hidden keystroke logger — we confirm by elimination).'


## Classic analog RF audio bugs / body wires / wireless microphones — continuous FM (and AM/subcarrier variant) carriers across low-band VHF through UHF

This class is the oldest and still most common eavesdropping threat: a microphone driving a simple RF oscillator that radiates a continuous carrier the operator listens to on a receiver/scanner. On the air, the distinguishing signature is a narrow (or wide) FM carrier that is UP CONTINUOUSLY whenever powered, with audio-correlated deviation — you can demodulate it and hear the room. Signatures differ meaningfully by (a) BAND segment the oscillator lands in, (b) DEVIATION/bandwidth: narrowband FM (~10-16 kHz occupied, crystal/quartz-stable) vs. standard narrow FM (~25-35 kHz occupied) vs. WIDE-deviation FM (broadcast-style, ~150-200+ kHz occupied), (c) crystal/PLL-locked (rock-steady) vs. free-running LC (visibly DRIFTS and warms), and (d) modulation flavor: plain FM, AM (rare/older), or a subcarrier/voice-inversion-scrambled FM. A thousand kit-bug PCBs that all free-run in 88-108 MHz are ONE signature; a quartz UHF 433 bodywire is a genuinely DIFFERENT signature. Rows are split by band x deviation-class x crystal/free-running because those are what change the detector's search. REACH is the defining operational constraint for this class. Two front-end floors matter: (1) a stock PlutoSDR's guaranteed floor is 325 MHz — so ALL of 30-216 MHz (low-band, the 88-108 FM-kit bug, mid-VHF pro bugs, 174-216 bodywires) is out of stock-Pluto reach and needs the AD9364 tuning-range unlock or a separate low-VHF front end (RTL-SDR/Airspy). Note the AD9364 trick only reaches down to ~70 MHz, so it recovers 88-216 MHz but NOT 30-50 MHz low-band. (2) The USRP B210's floor is 70 MHz, NOT DC — so the 30-50 MHz low-band row is BELOW the B210 too and needs an external downconverter/transverter even on the B210. Everything 70 MHz and up is in B210 reach; everything 325 MHz and up (UHF bodywires, 433/470/512/608) is in stock-Pluto reach; nothing in this class is above 6 GHz so neither the Pluto's 3.8/6 GHz ceiling nor the B210's 6 GHz ceiling is a limit here.

**How to confirm:**
- FM-demodulate the suspect carrier and listen: if you hear the room (your own voice/tap test correlates), it is almost certainly an audio bug, not a data or telemetry signal.
- Do a physical stimulus/correlation test: make a distinctive noise or play a tone in the room and watch the carrier's deviation (FM) or amplitude (AM) track it live on the waterfall.
- Watch the carrier over 5-15 minutes: a free-running bug DRIFTS/creeps in frequency as it warms; a crystal/PLL bug stays rock-steady. Records which signature row applies.
- Rule out legitimate emitters: real FM-broadcast stations carry program audio + RDS/pilot; real land-mobile/ham carries licensed traffic and keys intermittently. A 24/7 unlicensed carrier with live room audio and no ID is the flag.
- If FM-demod yields harsh/inverted (Donald-Duck) audio or a subcarrier tone, test a voice-inversion de-scrambler — confirms the scrambled-bug signature.
- For sub-325 MHz suspects a stock Pluto cannot see, confirm with an AD9364-unlocked Pluto or an HF/VHF-capable receiver (RTL-SDR/Airspy) before clearing 70-325 MHz. For 30-50 MHz low-band, note EVEN the B210 and AD9364-unlocked Pluto fall below ~70 MHz — use an external downconverter/transverter or an HF/low-VHF receiver. Do NOT declare 30-216 MHz clean off any stock-Pluto sweep, and do not assume the B210 covers 30-50 MHz.
- Power-down correlation: if you can kill room mains / suspect device power and the carrier disappears, that localizes a mains- or device-powered bug (battery bugs will persist).
- Direction-find / near-field sniff to localize: signal strength should peak at the physical device; combine with an NLJD for powered-off or dormant electronics.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Legitimate FM broadcast stations and their SCA subcarriers in 88-108 MHz — the single biggest false-positive source; discriminate by RDS/pilot + program audio.

- Baby monitors, wireless intercoms, and old analog cordless phones (some 43-50 MHz, 46-49 MHz, 900 MHz) — continuous or push-to-talk audio carriers that look bug-like; note the 43-50 MHz ones sit in the low-band row that no stock SDR here reaches.
- Wireless assistive-listening and tour-guide systems (72-76 MHz, 216-217 MHz) — continuous FM audio by design.
- Analog wireless-camera AUDIO subcarriers overlap the wide-deviation-FM row — but those belong to the video-bug class; don't double-count.
- RC/telemetry and other free-running LC oscillators that drift like a bug but carry no voice on demod.

**TSCM note:** Analog FM audio bugs are the highest-prior threat in a residential/family-law or SMB sweep because they are cheap, ubiquitous, and continuously radiating — the easiest thing to actually catch on a spectrum sweep IF you can reach the band. The operational gap for this kit is REACH, and it has two floors, not one. (1) A stock PlutoSDR's guaranteed floor is 325 MHz, so ALL of 30-216 MHz — the 88-108 MHz FM-band kit bug (the single most common hobby bug), mid-VHF pro bugs, and 174-216 MHz bodywires — is OUT of stock-Pluto reach. The AD9364 tuning-range unlock recovers roughly 70-325 MHz (so it does reach 88-216 MHz), but a stock-Pluto-only sweep MISSES the most common hobby bug entirely; this must be disclosed and covered with an AD9364-unlocked Pluto or a cheap RTL-SDR/Airspy for 24-1700 MHz before a band is called clean. (2) The 30-50 MHz low-band row is below ~70 MHz and therefore below the AD9364 unlock floor AND below the B210's 70 MHz floor — NEITHER a modded Pluto NOR a B210 reaches it directly; it needs an external downconverter/transverter or an HF/low-VHF receiver. The earlier claim that the B210 'covers 100% of this class' is wrong for exactly this row. The 325 MHz-and-up UHF bug rows (especially quartz 433/450-460 MHz micro-transmitters) are squarely in stock-Pluto reach and are where a Pluto-based tool is strongest. Nothing in this class is above 6 GHz, so no upper-frequency ceiling (Pluto 3.8/6 GHz or B210 6 GHz) is a limit here — the constraint is entirely at the low end. Best single discriminator across every row: a continuous, unlicensed carrier that FM/AM-demodulates to live room audio and correlates with a stimulus test — everything else (drift vs. stability, deviation width, scramble) just sorts it into the right signature row. Guard against over-alerting: professional wireless mics and FM broadcast are the dominant benign look-alikes and should be characterized (is it expected in this room?) rather than auto-flagged. When running an AD9364-unlocked Pluto below its 325 MHz spec floor, expect degraded sensitivity, images, and spurs — corroborate any sub-325 MHz hit rather than trusting a single spur.


## Smart-home mesh radios (Zigbee / Z-Wave / Thread / 802.15.4) repurposed as covert sensors

This class covers the low-rate mesh PHYs that carpet modern smart homes: Z-Wave (sub-GHz GFSK), Zigbee and Thread (both 2.4 GHz IEEE 802.15.4 O-QPSK/DSSS), and the less-common 802.15.4 sub-GHz PHYs (915 MHz BPSK and O-QPSK). For TSCM they matter because the exact same radios that run a legitimate door sensor or bulb can be flashed/repurposed to carry a covert PIR trip, a mic-VOX event, a keystroke count, or a beacon out through a homeowner's own hub — the traffic hides in plain sight among dozens of legitimate nodes. The distinct ON-AIR signatures are driven by BAND x MODULATION x CHANNEL WIDTH x DATA RATE, not by product. Z-Wave splits into three regional data-rate PHYs plus Z-Wave Long Range; Zigbee/Thread on 2.4 GHz share ONE PHY across 16 channels (one signature, not sixteen — the SDR sees identical O-QPSK/DSSS bursts, only the center frequency shifts); and the 915 MHz 802.15.4 band has two mutually-incompatible PHYs (BPSK vs O-QPSK). All Z-Wave/sub-GHz rows sit comfortably inside a stock PlutoSDR (325-3800 MHz). The 2.4 GHz rows sit at the very top edge of stock Pluto and squarely inside a B210. Nothing here exceeds 6 GHz. The dominant TSCM challenge is NOT detection — these bands are trivially busy — it is discrimination: telling a repurposed covert node from the legitimate mesh, which requires traffic-pattern and provenance analysis, not just a spectrum hit.

**How to confirm:**
- Enumerate the legitimate network from the homeowner's own controller: pull the full Z-Wave node list (node IDs, product IDs) and the Zigbee/Thread device inventory (PAN ID, short addresses, endpoints). Reconcile EVERY node against a device the homeowner can physically point to. Any unaccounted node is the finding — not the mere presence of the protocol.
- Discriminate by modulation family, not just band: in the 902-928 MHz band, Z-Wave = GFSK narrow, 802.15.4 sub-GHz = BPSK-DSSS or O-QPSK-DSSS wider, LoRa = chirp, FHSS telemetry = hopping. Classify the mystery signal before assuming it is a covert node.
- Capture protocol-layer traffic, not just IQ: use a real 802.15.4/Zigbee sniffer (CC2531/CC2652, nRF52840 dongle) and a Z-Wave sniffer (Zniffer/Silabs) to read frame headers, home/PAN IDs, and node addressing. IQ alone cannot attribute a node; frame provenance can.

- Power-down test: with owner consent, power off ALL known hubs/sensors and re-scan. Any 908/916/2405-2480/902-928 MHz mesh traffic that survives the shutdown is not part of the declared network — localize its source.

- Decode with a known-good 802.15.4 receiver (killerbee/zbdump on a CC2531, or GNU Radio ieee802-15-4 block) to confirm PHY and read PAN ID / short addresses; a live PAN ID confirms Zigbee/Thread vs. a bare carrier.

- Cross-reference every detected PAN/node against the client's declared device inventory; an 802.15.4 node with no owner-known counterpart is the actionable finding, not the mere presence of the band.
- Physically inspect suspected host objects - a covert 802.15.4 tap draws almost no power and hides in USB dongles, power strips, sensors, or wall warts; RF alone cannot distinguish covert from legitimate on this shared PHY.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Overwhelming legitimate presence: a normal smart home has DOZENS of legitimate Z-Wave/Zigbee/Thread nodes. Detection of the protocol is expected and by itself means nothing — this class is discrimination-limited, not detection-limited.
- 2.4 GHz band is shared with Wi-Fi, Bluetooth/BLE, and microwave leakage; Zigbee/Thread bursts sit in the gaps between Wi-Fi channels and can be masked by heavy Wi-Fi traffic or mistaken for BLE advertising bursts.
- 902-928 MHz ISM band is extremely crowded: LoRa/LoRaWAN, FHSS wireless telemetry, utility smart meters (which also burst in 902-928), wireless doorbells, and 900 MHz cordless-adjacent gear all share the band with Z-Wave and 802.15.4 sub-GHz. A 915 MHz hit is very likely a legitimate meter or LoRa node.
- Z-Wave classic vs Z-Wave LR vs 802.15.4-BPSK all live in the same 908-920 MHz neighborhood; misreading modulation can cross-label them.
- Thread and Zigbee are indistinguishable at the IQ/PHY layer (identical 802.15.4 2.4 GHz PHY) — you cannot tell a benign Nanoleaf-Thread mesh from a covert Thread node without frame-level capture.
- Multiprotocol hubs (Echo, Aqara M3, Homey Pro) legitimately emit on BOTH Z-Wave sub-GHz AND Zigbee/Thread 2.4 GHz, so a single device can create hits in multiple signature rows.
- Bluetooth 2.4 GHz spy cams/mics and Wi-Fi cameras are a DIFFERENT class; do not attribute their traffic to the mesh signatures — verify by bandwidth/modulation before labeling.
- The 2.4 GHz 802.15.4 signature is indistinguishable at RF from any legitimate Zigbee/Thread/Matter smart-home device - modern homes have dozens; presence is expected, not suspicious.
- BLE (1-2 MHz GFSK, adaptive hopping) and 802.15.4 both live in 2.4 GHz ISM; a wide-scan detector can confuse a wideband BLE burst with a narrow 802.15.4 channel edge - verify the ~2 MHz occupied width and 5 MHz raster.
- Wi-Fi 20 MHz channels overlap and can mask 802.15.4 channels 15/20/25 that sit between Wi-Fi 1/6/11; strong Wi-Fi can hide a low-power sensor burst.
- Sub-GHz 902-928 MHz is shared with LoRa/LoRaWAN, legacy 900 MHz cordless/telemetry, and many ISM remote controls - narrow FSK blips there are frequently benign non-Z-Wave devices.
- Wi-SUN/802.15.4g energy on 902-928 MHz is almost always the utility smart meter and is a routine environmental signal, not a threat.

**TSCM note:** Reach: every Z-Wave row (908.42 / 916 / 912 / 920 MHz) and both 915 MHz 802.15.4 sub-GHz PHYs (902-928 MHz) are comfortably inside a STOCK PlutoSDR's 325-3800 MHz tuning range — no AD9364 frequency-extension mod needed. The 2.4 GHz Zigbee/Thread signature (2405-2480 MHz) is within stock Pluto's range but sits well up the band where stock Pluto front-end sensitivity/filtering is weaker; a B210 covers it cleanly. NOTHING in this class is above 6 GHz, so the whole class is in-range for a B210 and (except for front-end margin at 2.4 GHz) for a stock Pluto. Sample-rate reality: the 2.4 GHz O-QPSK signature is ~2 MHz wide and the sub-GHz PHYs up to ~1.2 MHz — size your capture bandwidth accordingly; a stock Pluto at high sample rates over USB can drop samples, so verify no overflow when hunting fast Zigbee bursts.

Operational reality for TSCM: this is the hardest class in the kit to act on because the signatures are ubiquitous and legitimate. A spectrum-analyzer hit is the START of the investigation, never the conclusion. The real deliverable is RECONCILIATION — every mesh node accounted for against a physical, owner-acknowledged device — plus the power-down survivor test and stimulus-correlation walk. Prioritize the covert-friendly niches: Z-Wave Long Range (high power, reaches off-property collectors), and the 915 MHz 802.15.4 sub-GHz PHYs (uncommon, good wall penetration, missed by 2.4-GHz-only sweeps). Any GFSK/PSK mesh burst that survives a full known-device shutdown, or that direction-finds to a spot with no declared device, is the report-worthy finding. IQ capture alone cannot attribute these; pair the SDR with protocol-layer 802.15.4 and Z-Wave sniffers. ## Wi-Fi / IP hidden cameras (802.11 a/b/g/n/ac/ax/ah, all bands + SoftAP setup mode)

Wi-Fi/IP hidden cameras are the single most common covert-camera class in the field, and on the air they are indistinguishable from any other Wi-Fi station: the camera is just an 802.11 client (or, in setup mode, a temporary AP) pushing an uplink-heavy H.264/H.265 stream. The distinct RF signatures are defined by the 802.11 PHY variant and channel plan, NOT by the disguise: 2.4 GHz (20/40 MHz), 5 GHz U-NII-1..3 (20/40/80/160 MHz), 6 GHz Wi-Fi 6E, 900 MHz 802.11ah HaLow for long-range/battery IoT cams, plus the SoftAP setup-mode beaconing that many cams emit on first boot or when they lose their saved network. Because a camera looks like a phone, laptop, or thermostat at the antenna, the discriminator for TSCM is behavioral (persistent uplink-dominant traffic from a fixed location), not spectral. REACH: stock ADALM-Pluto (325-3800 MHz, AD9363) reaches 900 MHz HaLow and the 2.4 GHz band cleanly; all 5 GHz signatures need the AD9364 firmware hack (reports as AD9364, extends to ~70 MHz-6 GHz), while a USRP B210 tunes 70 MHz-6 GHz natively and needs NO hack for 5 GHz (only appropriate antenna/filtering). 6 GHz Wi-Fi 6E (5925-7125) sits at the very top edge of both the AD9364-hacked Pluto and the B210 (both nominally ~6 GHz), so upper 6 GHz U-NII channels are effectively out of reach for both. 40/80/160 MHz channel widths meet or exceed the Pluto's practical instantaneous bandwidth (nominal 20 MHz, up to ~56 MHz degraded) and the B210's ~56 MHz (61.44 MHz max), so wide channels can only be reliably detected as energy/occupancy via swept scans, not fully captured in one shot.

- For Wi-Fi (2.4/5 GHz): DO NOT rely on SDR alone — put a Wi-Fi NIC in monitor mode (Kismet/airodump), enumerate clients, match OUI to a camera vendor, and look for a fixed device with steady high-rate uplink video. Power-cycle the suspect and watch the station drop off.
- Universal physical confirm: sweep with a directional antenna or near-field probe to localize the emitter to a physical spot, then visually inspect for a baby-monitor camera/mic unit. A found monitor in a room where no infant is present is the finding — document make/model/FCC ID.
- Correlate to a receiver: baby monitors are paired transmitter+parent-unit. Finding the parent/receiver unit (or evidence the feed is being watched) elevates it from 'benign leftover' to 'active surveillance.'

**Might legitimately be:**
- 49 MHz: old cordless phones, 49 MHz walkie-talkies, RC toys/models, and wireless intercoms share the exact channels — an FM carrier here is not automatically a bug until audio-correlated.
- 420-430 MHz: 70 cm amateur radio, 433 MHz ISM remotes/sensors, and analog FPV video downlinks overlap; only the wide FM-video hump (not narrowband hits) points to a camera.
- 902-928 MHz: 900 MHz cordless phones, wireless headphones/speakers, Z-Wave, LoRa, smart meters, and industrial telemetry all live here — heavy benign traffic.
- 1880-1930 MHz DECT: cordless phone bases and handsets use the identical PHY (EU 1880-1900, NA UPCS 1920-1930). A DECT channel is far more likely a household phone than a bug — physical localization is essential.
- 2.4 GHz analog video: analog wireless CCTV, drone/FPV video, and old wireless AV senders (senders for TV) produce the same FM-video hump.
- 2.4 GHz FHSS: Bluetooth/BLE, wireless mice/keyboards, wireless game controllers, Zigbee, and countless proprietary IoT links flood this band — a huge false-positive pool.
- 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi: every phone, laptop, tablet, smart TV, Wi-Fi security camera, doorbell, and IoT device — RF layer cannot distinguish a Wi-Fi baby monitor from any of them.
- 5 GHz Wi-Fi: every dual-band laptop/phone, streaming stick, mesh node, and 5 GHz security camera. Radar/DFS activity in U-NII-2 can also appear.
- General: a baby monitor is a LEGITIMATE device. Its RF signature is 'benign consumer electronics' by design. The TSCM finding is context (monitor present where it shouldn't be / aimed at a target), not an inherently illicit waveform.

**TSCM note:** Baby monitors are the archetypal 'hidden in plain sight' bug: legitimate, always-on, and cheap. Triage by generation. (1) ANALOG generations (49 MHz FM audio, 420/900/2.4 GHz FM video, 900 MHz FM audio) are the high-value finds — they can be DEMODULATED IN THE FIELD to plaintext audio/video, so a carrier that resolves to the target room's audio or picture is near-conclusive evidence of live surveillance. Prioritize these; they are also the ones a hostile party would repurpose precisely because they are unencrypted and receivable on any matching consumer receiver. (2) The 49 MHz generation is the single most important reason NOT to trust a stock Pluto sweep alone for this class — it is below the Pluto/B210 floor and will be MISSED entirely unless you add a VHF-capable receiver (RTL-SDR/RSP/scanner). Document this gap in the sweep report. (3) DIGITAL generations (DECT, 900 MHz/2.4 GHz FHSS, Wi-Fi) cannot be demodulated to plaintext in the field and share their band with dozens of benign devices, so they demand pattern-of-life + physical localization + (for Wi-Fi) network-layer identification. Do not call a DECT channel or a Wi-Fi burst a bug from the spectrum alone. NOTE for DECT: NA 'DECT 6.0' occupies the UPCS band 1920-1930 MHz with 5 carriers; EU DECT occupies 1880-1900 MHz with 10 carriers — sweep the correct span for the region. (4) The Wi-Fi generations blur completely into the 'Wi-Fi spy camera' class — treat them there for identification; the only baby-monitor-specific tells are OUI (VTech/Motorola/Nanit/Owlet/Cubo/Miku ranges) and a fixed device streaming steady uplink video. (5) LEGAL/CONTEXT flag: a working baby monitor pointed at a bedroom/bathroom, or a monitor whose paired parent-unit is off-premises, moves this from 'harmless leftover' toward potential unlawful interception/voyeurism — surface it to the client and, where warranted, counsel immediately rather than just logging a signal. (6) Always recover make/model/FCC ID from the physical unit; it resolves the band/PHY ambiguity instantly and supports any downstream report.


## DECT (Digital Enhanced Cordless Telecommunications) — cordless phones, DECT bugs/audio taps, DECT baby monitors, and DECT ULE sensors, across regional 1.88-1.93 GHz band placements plus DECT-2020 NR+

DECT is a single physical-layer family that is trivially in reach of a stock Pluto (325-3800 MHz) and a B210 — all variants live around 1.88-1.93 GHz. Classic DECT / CAT-iq / DECT ULE all share ONE on-air PHY: GFSK at 1.152 Mbit/s (~288 kHz peak deviation, modulation index ~0.5), carriers spaced 1.728 MHz, and a 24-slot TDMA/TDD frame repeating every exactly 10 ms (so bursts recur at 100 Hz / multiples). Note the ~1.728 MHz figure is the CHANNEL SPACING; a single DECT burst's occupied (-3 dB) bandwidth is narrower, roughly 1.1-1.4 MHz, so carriers do not fully fill their 1.728 MHz lane. That 10 ms TDMA heartbeat with ~1.7 MHz-wide bursts on a 1.728 MHz grid is the master DECT fingerprint and is what the matcher should key on. The genuinely DISTINCT signatures are therefore NOT per-product but per (a) REGIONAL BAND PLACEMENT — EU/RoW 1880-1900, US/Canada 1920-1930, Brazil 1910-1920, LatAm-ex-Brazil 1910-1930, Japan 1893.5-1906.1, India 1880-1930 — because that changes where the SDR tunes and detects; (b) DUTY PATTERN — a live cordless CALL is near-continuous TDMA on 1-2 carriers, an IDLE/standby base emits a low-rate dummy-bearer beacon (~every 10 ms but only a couple slots), and a DECT ULE sensor is bursty/sleepy (long silence, short DECT burst on event/keepalive); and (c) DECT-2020 NR+, which is a totally different modulation (CP-OFDM, scalable 1.728-13.824 MHz channels) sharing the same band. For TSCM: a covert DECT audio bug or a DECT baby monitor used as a room monitor looks IDENTICAL on the air to a legitimate cordless phone/base — the discriminator is location and persistence, not RF class. DECT is a favored covert-audio band precisely because sweepers who only cover 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and analog bands miss it. Everything here is inside stock-Pluto reach; nothing needs the AD9364 mod or exceeds B210's 6 GHz ceiling.

The strongest operational tell is that a powered DECT base emits a periodic dummy-bearer beacon every 10 ms even when idle, so a hidden bug betrays itself at rest — sweep the applicable regional window with a directional antenna and hunt the beacon to the physical object. Always reconcile every DECT emitter against a physical inventory of known phones, monitors, and smart-home hubs; the risk item is any DECT signature that does not map to an accounted-for device. Note that DECT audio bugs are commonly marketed alongside GSM/4G listening devices; if you find DECT, also sweep the cellular uplink bands for a co-located GSM bug. This is all internal sweep methodology — a client-facing report should state findings (device present/located or clear) in plain language and avoid implying audio content was intercepted.

## Carrier-current bugs (audio/data modulated onto AC mains and building low-voltage wiring) + hardwired microphone runs

Carrier-current bugs impress audio (or digitized audio) onto the building's own AC power wiring as a conducted subcarrier, typically 20-500 kHz (classic LF band) and 1.6-7.5 MHz (modern HF "PLC" band), so the mains simultaneously powers the device and carries the signal to a receiver plugged into the same electrical phase downstream of the service transformer. This is fundamentally a CONDUCTED / near-field problem, not a free-space RF one: the intended signal lives on the copper, and only leaks weakly by radiation/induction from the wiring. Almost NONE of it is reliably free-space-detectable by an antenna-fed SDR, and worse, the entire useful band sits BELOW a stock ADALM-Pluto's 325 MHz floor and (for the LF portion) below a B210's 70 MHz floor. Real detection of this class is done with a mains-coupled probe / VLF-HF conducted receiver (e.g. AC line coupler into a 10 kHz-30 MHz receiver, ANDRE-class near-field probe, or dedicated carrier-current detectors like the REI/Kaiser SCD-5, MLK-2030), plus physical inspection of outlets, junction boxes, and ceiling voids. The closely-related hardwired-mic-run threat radiates essentially nothing and is a physical-search / audio-emanation (WMD-2000 acoustic-stimulation) problem exclusively. The distinct on-the-wire signatures below differ by band, modulation (FM analog vs OOK vs OFDM vs GMSK vs spread-spectrum) and duty, but the operative TSCM reality is uniform: match at the mains coupler, not the antenna.

A stock ADALM-Pluto (325-3800 MHz) cannot reach any of it; a B210 (70 MHz-6 GHz) reaches none of the LF band and only marginal radiated leakage of the 2-30 MHz HF/BPL band. More importantly the signal is CONDUCTED on the copper, so an antenna-fed radio of any kind is the wrong instrument except for sniffing weak near-field leakage a few inches from the wiring. Treat carrier-current and wired-mic threats as a conducted/near-field + physical-search workstream, run in parallel with (not replaced by) the free-space SDR sweep: (1) mains-coupled VLF-HF receiver or dedicated carrier-current detector; (2) near-field probe walked along wiring; (3) hands-on inspection of every outlet, box, strip and ceiling void; (4) TDR/tone trace and acoustic-stimulation detector for baseband hardwired runs. Because these devices are powered by and hidden inside the electrical system they can run 24/7 indefinitely with no battery to die, and the analog ones give up plaintext room audio to anyone downstream on the same phase — a high-severity, low-tech, easily-overlooked threat. Localization to a physical outlet/pair is the deliverable; a spectrum screenshot is not. Flag any unknown powerline-networking adapter as a hardware finding to be physically verified rather than dismissed as normal networking.


## GPS/GNSS jammers, repeaters, and spoofers (anti-tracker RF devices)

This class attacks the GNSS bands (GPS L1 1575.42, L2 1227.6, L5 1176.45 MHz; GLONASS G1 FDMA ~1598-1609 centered near 1602; BeiDou B1I ~1561.098). The overwhelmingly common real-world device is the cheap 12V "cigarette-lighter" jammer: a single microwave VCO swept by a 555-timer sawtooth (~133 kHz sweep rate confirmed in the Hackaday teardown) that smears a few MHz of energy across L1. Because genuine GNSS arrives BELOW the receiver noise floor (~-130 dBm), the tell is inverted from most surveillance work: instead of a hidden weak emitter, the operator finds a STRONG carrier/noise sitting exactly on a GNSS band where there should be nothing but thermal noise. Three behaviorally distinct sub-families share these bands: (1) JAMMERS - swept-noise or CW that raise the floor and deny GNSS; (2) REPEATERS/re-radiators - legitimate-looking near-noise live GNSS re-broadcast indoors (hangars/labs), also abusable to feed a fixed benign fix or keep a tracker "alive"; (3) SPOOFERS - SDR (HackRF/Pluto/bladeRF) transmitting structured counterfeit GNSS just above noise to move a receiver's reported position. All GNSS carriers fall inside a stock Pluto's 325-3800 MHz range (L5 at 1176 is the lowest and well in reach). IMPORTANT RF REALITY CHECK: the single-VCO cheap jammer teardown shows a tuning range of ~1466-1590 MHz - it covers GPS L1 and BeiDou B1 but does NOT reach GLONASS at 1602; genuine GLONASS/wide-block coverage requires a wider-tuning or multi-VCO/multi-antenna unit, so a raised-noise plateau extending past ~1590 up to 1610 implies a more capable device, not the cheapest one. The only out-of-band member is the LoJack 173.075 MHz stolen-vehicle-recovery band that some "GPS+LoJack" combo jammers also hit, which is below the stock Pluto floor. A directional yagi/patch and near-field sniffing at the vehicle lighter/OBD/dash is the practical hunt because the jammer is line-powered and physically present.

**How to confirm:**
- Baseline first: with the suspect device OFF (or before entering the vehicle), capture the GNSS bands. Genuine L1/L2/L5 look like flat thermal noise - GNSS is BELOW the noise floor and invisible on a Pluto/B210 without correlation. Any strong hump or tone there while 'off' means something else is on.
- Restore normal state and watch L1 (1575.42): a broad swept hump, a wide 1560-1615 shelf, or a CW spike appearing is the jammer signature. Note bandwidth, upper edge (does it reach GLONASS ~1602?), and whether it sweeps - upper-edge >1590 implies a more capable multi-VCO unit than the cheapest class.
- Confirm line-powered and local: kill/unplug the 12V lighter, USB ports, and OBD-II port one at a time; the emission dropping when a port is unpowered pinpoints a lighter/OBD/USB jammer.
- Direction-find with a yagi/patch antenna toward the dash, center console, OBD port, and under-seat area - the source is physically present, so signal strength peaks sharply at the device.
- Ground-truth with an actual GNSS receiver (phone GNSS test app, u-blox, or dedicated probe): loss of fix / C/No collapse across many satellites at once = jamming; a confident but WRONG fix = spoofing; a clean fix with no sky view = re-radiation.
- Distinguish spoof vs jam: spoofing leaves L1 looking like real GPS but a few dB hot and yields a false position; jamming raises the whole floor and yields no fix. Check reported position against known truth.
- For the 173 MHz LoJack channel, switch to an RTL-SDR / RF Explorer / B210 (below Pluto's 325 MHz floor) to confirm the narrowband emitter if a combo GPS+LoJack unit is suspected.
- Physically recover the device: cigarette-lighter jammers are small pucks with 1-6 stub antennas; check the lighter socket, OBD port, USB hubs, and glovebox. Preserve chain of custody - possession/operation is itself an offense.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Legitimate GNSS repeaters/re-radiators in hangars, parking structures, test labs, and vehicle-assembly plants intentionally re-broadcast real L1 indoors - spectrally identical to a benign or malicious repeater; context (is this a facility that installs them?) decides.
- Active GPS antennas / LNAs can self-oscillate or leak, putting a small carrier near L1 that mimics a weak CW jammer.
- Iridium subscriber downlink (1616-1626.5 MHz) sits just above the GLONASS band and is a legitimate strong, bursty signal - do NOT mistake it for GLONASS jamming; it is above 1616 and time-slotted, not a continuous GNSS-centered hump. Iridium is a known real-world nuisance source of L-band interference near GNSS.
- Inmarsat / MSS L-band services and some aeronautical links occupy nearby L-band and are legitimate; a jammer sits centered ON the GNSS carriers, legitimate services sit in adjacent allocations.
- Harmonics/intermod from strong cellular (LTE ~1.9-2.1 GHz) or a broadband PA can drop energy into L-band; verify the emission tracks a GNSS center frequency and is local to the vehicle, not a distant tower.
- Poorly shielded electronics (some dashcams, cheap DC-DC converters, LED drivers) emit broadband hash that can raise the L1 floor without being a jammer - looks like weak wideband noise spanning far more than the GNSS band, not a clean swept hump.
- GNSS spoofing is nearly indistinguishable from legitimate GPS on a spectrum view alone - it MUST be confirmed by a receiver reporting a false position, not by RF appearance.

**TSCM note:** Practical field notes: (1) The inverted-tell principle is the crux - real GNSS is BELOW the noise floor, so a Pluto/B210 doing raw spectrum normally sees NOTHING at L1/L2/L5. Anything strong there is by definition anomalous and almost always a jammer, repeater, or spoofer. Teach operators not to expect to 'see GPS.' (2) RF REALITY on cheap units: the dominant $15-40 single-VCO lighter jammer (Hackaday teardown: NE555 ~133 kHz sawtooth driving a ~1466-1590 MHz VCO) covers GPS L1 and BeiDou B1 but NOT GLONASS at 1602 - so genuine GLONASS/wide-block coverage or L2/L5 presence signals a more capable multi-VCO/multi-antenna device and upgrades the sophistication/intent assessment. Don't assume every 'all-GNSS' advertised claim is real; verify the actual upper edge. (3) These devices are line-powered and co-located with the target (lighter, OBD, USB, or room outlet), so DF and physical search beat spectral subtleties - find the puck. (4) Effect first, RF second: cheapest confirmation is a second GNSS receiver losing lock (jam) or holding a wrong-but-confident position (spoof). (5) Legal exposure is significant and asymmetric - merely possessing/operating a jammer violates 47 U.S.C. and FCC rules in the US (and equivalents in CA/UK/EU); if one is found in a client matter, flag it as evidence of an offense, not just an RF nuisance, and preserve chain of custody. (6) Spoofing is the higher-threat, lower-visibility case (a fleet/asset mis-tracked rather than dark-tracked) and needs a TCXO-stable SDR; validate against ground truth. (7) The 173 MHz LoJack channel and any sub-325 MHz content need a different front end (RTL-SDR/RF Explorer, or B210 which reaches 70 MHz); a stock Pluto cannot see it.


## Pro/prosumer wireless microphone systems (bodypack/plug-on/clip-on transmitters) used covertly for audio surveillance

Wireless mic systems are one of the most common "accidental" and deliberate covert audio-surveillance tools a TSCM sweep encounters: a bodypack or plug-on transmitter with a lav mic is a body-wearable, continuously-keyed audio bug that is fully legal to buy and leaves a clean, well-behaved RF signature. On the air they fall into four primary regime families, each with distinct signatures: (1) UHF ~470-698 MHz analog FM, a narrow (~130-200 kHz) continuous FM carrier — the classic wireless-mic look and the single most recognizable signature; (2) UHF ~470-698 MHz digital (Shure SLX-D/ULX-D/Axient, Sennheiser EW-D, AKG DMS800) — a continuous or near-continuous digitally-modulated carrier still inside the 200 kHz TV-band channel mask (ETSI EN 300 422 caps occupied bandwidth at 200 kHz below 1 GHz, 600 kHz above 1 GHz); (3) 900 MHz ISM (902-928) license-free digital, older Line 6 and Sennheiser/AKG 900 systems; (4) 2.4 GHz digital, frequency-hopping spread-spectrum (Rode Wireless GO/DJI Mic-class, Shure GLXD, Line 6 XD-V, Audio-Technica System 10, Sennheiser XSW-D) — bursty FHSS that shares the band with Wi-Fi/BT and is the hardest to distinguish from consumer traffic. Plus legacy VHF (72-216 MHz) analog and a 1.8-1.9 GHz DECT-style regime. Every regime above VHF is inside a stock ADALM-Pluto's 325-3800 MHz tuning range; none is above 6 GHz. The dominant TSCM challenge is not reach but discrimination: a UHF FM mic in a hotel or church AV rack is a legitimate device, and a 2.4 GHz hopping mic is nearly indistinguishable from Wi-Fi/BT without demodulating audio. Distinct signatures are separated by band, channel width, modulation and duty; the "hundreds of products" all collapse onto these few on-air fingerprints. Note on the US band map: after the 2020 incentive auction, US wireless-mic operation is effectively limited to below 608 MHz (plus 614-616 MHz and narrow duplex-gap slivers) — 617-698 MHz is now licensed 600 MHz cellular where mic use is prohibited — so 470-698 is used here as a DETECTION scan range (legacy gear + non-US regions still use up to ~694 MHz), not a legal-operation range.

**How to confirm:**
- Confirm continuous keying: a covert worn mic stays transmitting the whole time it is on. Watch the carrier/hopper over 1-2 minutes — a mic streams audio nonstop (even during silence for digital), unlike PTT radios or bursty data. A signal that dies when you leave a room and returns when you re-enter tracks a person.
- On UHF/VHF analog FM carriers, demodulate WFM then NFM in-app and LISTEN. If you hear clear room audio / your own footsteps / a colleague talking, it is an active analog wireless mic — this is the single most decisive confirmation and it is unencrypted by design.
- Analog vs digital shape check: analog FM carrier is peaky and 'breathes' (sidebands widen with speech volume); digital mic is a flat-topped noise-like block of constant amplitude with hard edges. Use the app's persistence/waterfall to see the difference.
- Physical near-field bring-up: walk a broadband/near-field detector or the SDR with a short antenna toward suspected persons/objects; a worn bodypack shows rising received power as you approach the wearer, confirming a body-worn source rather than a rack-mounted AV system.

- For 2.4 GHz FHSS: run a timed hop-cadence capture. A wireless mic hops with a steady period locked to its audio frame rate and never stops while on; compare against known Wi-Fi (wide, bursty, tied to your traffic) and BLE (advertising cadence). Correlate persistence with a physical sweep.
- Rule out the room's legitimate AV: check for an obvious receiver (rack, camera hot-shoe RX, presenter kit). If a receiver and its paired transmitter are accounted for and inventoried, log as authorized; an UNPAIRED transmitter with no known receiver on premises is the red flag.
- Cross-band sweep: because the same product line ships in UHF, 900, 1.9 GHz and 2.4 GHz variants, sweep all four regimes (plus VHF if AD9364-modded) before clearing a space — do not clear on a single-band pass.
- Watch the US band map: a strong 'mic-like' carrier above 608 MHz (up to 698) in the US is either legacy/illegal mic gear or is actually 600 MHz cellular/other service — verify before logging it as a mic; that segment is no longer licensed for mic operation.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Legitimate venue AV: hotels, churches, courtrooms, conference centers and classrooms run banks of UHF analog/digital wireless mics — a keyed mic carrier is FAR more often authorized house AV than a bug. Inventory the room's own gear first.
- UHF-TV-band overlap: 470-698 MHz also carries DTV broadcast, wireless IEM (in-ear monitor) packs, white-space devices, and (in the US 617-698 MHz) licensed 600 MHz cellular. IEMs look nearly identical (companded FM in the same band) but transmit FROM the rack TO the performer — direction and pairing distinguish them.
- 2.4 GHz is a swamp: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth/BLE, Zigbee, wireless keyboards/mice, drones, and consumer cameras all share 2400-2483.5 MHz. A hopping 2.4 GHz mic is genuinely hard to separate from BT/Wi-Fi without cadence analysis or audio confirmation — expect false positives here and lean on persistence + physical correlation.
- 900 MHz ISM overlap: 902-928 also holds cordless phones, LoRa/IoT, some baby monitors, RFID and utility telemetry — a 900 MHz digital block is not self-evidently a mic.
- 1.8-1.9 GHz overlap: DECT cordless phones and DECT-based room conferencing systems share the exact TDMA signature of DECT-based wireless mics; near-impossible to separate on RF alone.
- VHF overlap: 72-76 MHz assistive-listening transmitters, 169-172 MHz business paging/data, and 174-216 MHz VHF-TV broadcast all share the low-VHF FM look — a VHF FM carrier is not proof of a covert mic.
- IEM / assistive listening confusion: hearing-assist transmitters and IEM systems use the same companded-FM look; a continuous FM carrier is not proof of a covert mic by itself.
- Pilot-tone and comb-of-carriers patterns also appear in legitimate multi-channel productions — a comb of evenly spaced UHF carriers is normal in a working AV rack.
- Adjacent class (not covered here): the classic 88-108 MHz FM-broadcast-band 'spy bug' micro-transmitter and 49 MHz toy mics are a SEPARATE covert-audio device class (bare FM bug transmitter), not a wireless-mic SYSTEM — do not expect this class's fingerprints to match those; sweep the FM broadcast band under that class.

**TSCM note:** Covert-use reality: a wireless-mic bodypack or clip-on transmitter is the most 'deniable' audio bug because it is a legal, off-the-shelf pro-audio product — nothing on the RF says 'spy.' The threat model is a transmitter worn on a person, hidden in a bag/jacket, or planted near a target (taped under a table, inside a prop) with the paired receiver elsewhere. The tiny form factors matter for TSCM: Shure ADX1M micro-bodypack, Rode/DJI/Hollyland self-contained clip-ons, and onboard-recording packs (Zaxcom TRX) can be concealed easily and, in the recording case, may capture audio even with the RF link idle. Encrypted digital (SLX-D/ULX-D/Axient, EW-D, DMS800, AVX, and all the 2.4 GHz kits) yields NO clear audio — you can only prove presence and behavior, never content, from the SDR; the confirmation there is physical discovery. Legal/consent flag for the operator: in one-party-consent jurisdictions a person wearing their own mic may be lawful; a planted transmitter recording others without consent likely is not — surface any suspected unlawful interception to the client/counsel rather than adjudicating it in the field. Reach summary: the four regimes above VHF (UHF 470-698, 900 ISM, 1.8-1.9 GHz, 2.4 GHz) are ALL inside a stock ADALM-Pluto's 325-3800 MHz range; only the legacy VHF (72-216 MHz) analog mics fall below Pluto and need the AD9364 mod or a VHF front end. Nothing in this class is above 6 GHz, so the B210 covers it all; B210's larger instantaneous bandwidth mainly helps see the full 83.5 MHz of the 2.4 GHz band without sweeping. ## BLE item trackers (AirTag / Tile / Samsung SmartTag / Google Find My Device tags / Chipolo / Pebblebee, etc.) plus their UWB precision-finding radios

This class covers consumer "lost-item" tags and the phones/keyfobs that range to them. On the air there are really only a few PHYs: (1) Bluetooth Low Energy advertising, GFSK ~1 Msym/s, ~2 MHz occupied, transmitted on the three fixed advertising channels 37/38/39 (2402/2426/2480 MHz) — every mainstream tracker (AirTag, Tile, SmartTag, Google FMDN tags, Chipolo, Pebblebee, Eufy, Cube) uses this identical modulation; (2) the same GFSK on the 37 general-purpose DATA channels once connected/paired; (3) optional BLE Coded PHY (LE Long Range, spread GFSK) used by some newer tags/scanners; and (4) 802.15.4z HRP impulse-radio UWB for precision finding on channel 5 (6489.6 MHz) and channel 9 (7987.2 MHz), 499.2 MHz wide. CRITICAL for a spectrum matcher: the tracker "brands" (Find My, Tile, SmartTag, FMDN) are NOT distinct RF signatures — they are the SAME 2 MHz GFSK burst on ch 37/38/39. What separates them is the payload (manufacturer-data company ID / service-data UUID), which a spectrum-only tool CANNOT see — it needs a protocol-aware BLE sniffer (nRF Sniffer, Ubertooth, Sniffle) to read the advertising bytes. I list the shared GFSK air signature by advertising channel (what a Pluto/B210 can actually tune and detect), then break out the identifiable service-data tells as protocol-layer rows so the app can label a decoded advert, plus the two UWB rows flagged as at/above the B210 6 GHz edge. RANGE NOTE: all BLE is at 2.4 GHz — WELL below a stock Pluto's 325-3800 MHz upper limit, so 2.4 GHz BLE is OUT of a stock Pluto's reach and needs the AD9364 firmware mod (to ~6 GHz) or a B210 (70 MHz-6 GHz). Both UWB channels sit at/above the 6 GHz top of the B210 and are effectively out of range.

- For rotating-ID tags (AirTag, FMDN, SmartTag) do NOT try to lock a fixed MAC — it rotates ~every 15 min. Instead correlate by RSSI rising/falling with proximity and by the beacon reappearing after each rotation while the target moves.
- Physically localize with the RSSI gradient: sweep the antenna over seats, bags, wheel wells, bumpers, clothing, and gifts; the beacon RSSI peaks at the tag. Confirm by removing/bagging the suspected item and verifying the beacon disappears.
- Trigger the tag's own tells: an AirTag separated from its owner emits an audible chirp after a period, and NFC-tapping an AirTag/most Find My tags to a phone opens a page with the serial/partial owner info — capture it for the report.
- Do NOT expect to catch UWB on a Pluto or B210 — channel 5 (6489.6 MHz) is above the B210's 6 GHz edge and channel 9 (7987.2 MHz) is well out of range; UWB also only fires during an active precision-finding session, so it is not a passive-detection path.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Every other BLE device shares the exact ch37/38/39 GFSK PHY: phones, earbuds (AirPods, Galaxy Buds), smartwatches, fitness bands, BLE beacons, retail Eddystone/iBeacon transmitters, TV remotes, medical wearables, car keyfobs, smart-home sensors. Spectrum alone CANNOT tell a tracker from any of these — payload decode is mandatory.
- 0xFEAA is shared between Google FMDN trackers and plain Eddystone beacons (retail/proximity marketing) — the UUID alone is not proof of a tracker; the frame type must be decoded.
- AirPods, MacBooks, and any 'Find My'-enabled Apple accessory emit the same 0x004C manufacturer data as an AirTag, so an Apple-ID advert is not automatically a hidden tag — it may be the subject's own earbuds.
- The 500 MHz noise-like UWB impulse can be mistaken for wideband noise or another 6-8 GHz emitter; conversely legitimate UWB (car digital keys, smart-home ranging, phones unlocking) shares the same 802.15.4z waveform.
- A moving vehicle inherently carries a cloud of legitimate BLE advertisers (infotainment, TPMS-adjacent, phones), so a positive on the advertising channels in a car is expected and not by itself incriminating — you must isolate a persistent, owner-separated, following beacon.
- OpenHaystack / DIY firmware can make a generic nRF52 or ESP32 dev board advertise a valid Find My payload, so a decoded Apple-format advert does not guarantee genuine Apple hardware.

**TSCM note:** Practical hierarchy for this class: the reliable, always-on detection surface is BLE advertising GFSK on 2402/2426/2480 MHz — hunt there. A stock ADALM-Pluto (325-3800 MHz) DOES reach 2.4 GHz, so it can see the GFSK bursts as IQ energy, but it cannot decode which tracker it is; you need a protocol sniffer (nRF Sniffer, Ubertooth, Sniffle) to read the manufacturer/service tell and to catch the DULT 'separated from owner' flag. Treat the brand rows above as protocol-layer labels, not separate air signatures — physically they are the same 2 MHz burst. Rotating identifiers (Apple, Google, Samsung) defeat MAC-based tracking, so localize by RSSI + movement correlation, then confirm physically (NFC tap for serial, forced chirp, bag-and-verify-disappearance). UWB (ch5 6489.6 MHz, ch9 7987.2 MHz, ~500 MHz wide) is a red herring for passive sweeping: it is above/at the B210's 6 GHz ceiling, far beyond the Pluto (even AD9364-modded to ~6 GHz), and only transmits during an active precision-finding session — it will not lead you to a hidden tag. Report language for clients should distinguish 'a tracker-format beacon was detected and decoded' (high confidence, cite the UUID/company-ID tell and the owner-separated flag) from 'BLE energy present on advertising channels' (low confidence — could be any BLE device). Finally, the leading real-world risk here is stalking/IPV: if a following owner-separated tracker is confirmed on a client's person or vehicle, treat it as a potential safety and criminal matter (surveil/stalk statutes), preserve the device and its serial/NFC data rather than destroying it, and advise the client on law-enforcement and personal-safety steps.

## Satellite messengers / trackers for covert off-grid comms and tracking (Iridium, Globalstar, Inmarsat L-band uplink emitters)

This class covers battery-powered devices that reach orbiting satellites directly on L-band mobile-satellite-service (MSS) uplinks, so they work with zero cellular/Wi-Fi infrastructure — which is exactly what makes them attractive for covert tracking and off-grid comms in a TSCM context. The three networks a stock PlutoSDR (325-3800 MHz) can actually see on their UPLINK are Iridium (1616-1626.5 MHz), Globalstar (1610-1626.5 MHz), and Inmarsat (1626.5-1660.5 MHz). Detection requires long dwell / persistent monitoring on the uplink bands, near-field antenna work, and correlation with a reporting schedule. Also critical for TSCM: you can only hear these when the hidden device actually transmits its uplink; the far more common satellite-band energy near you is legitimate GPS/GNSS (1176/1227/1575 MHz) and satphone/BGAN traffic, so band presence alone is not proof of a bug. The distinct on-air signatures are relatively few (network + link-type), but the product layer is large (dozens of trackers/messengers all share the same handful of signatures). - Iridium's rapidly-changing TDMA bursts can momentarily resemble other TDMA/frequency-hopping sources; without the 41.667 kHz raster and ~90 ms framing, do not assume Iridium.
- LEO/GEO satellite DOWNLINKS and other space-to-earth signals in adjacent bands (e.g. Inmarsat/BGAN downlink 1525-1559 MHz, Iridium/Globalstar downlinks) are not device emissions and should not be flagged as a nearby bug.

**TSCM note:** Why this class matters for TSCM: satellite messengers/trackers are the go-to for covert tracking where a cellular GPS tracker would be jammed, out of coverage, or easily geofence-detected — they need no local network, work in wilderness/open-sky, and report straight to orbit. The classic threat is a magnetic or hardwired Globalstar SPOT Trace / SmartOne / Iridium-9603-based unit on a vehicle or in a bag, reporting a target's location to a third party. Two-way Iridium messengers (inReach/ZOLEO) can also be used for covert off-grid comms by a party who does not want to be on the cellular network. DETECTION REALITY: the hard part is not the frequency (all in easy Pluto reach) but the DUTY CYCLE — these emit for tens of milliseconds, minutes-to-hours apart, and only when they report or the user sends. Persistent/long-dwell monitoring and near-field antenna hunting during a live report are essential; a walk-through spectrum snapshot will miss them. Physical search of vehicles/assets during a known or forced report window is often more productive than pure RF. LEGAL/ETHICS: if a sweep turns up a satellite tracker the client did not place, treat it as potential evidence of unlawful tracking/stalking — preserve it in place where possible, photograph, document, and advise the client on law-enforcement involvement (per Matt's standing preference to surface suspected criminal exposure immediately). Chain of custody matters. HONEST LIMITS: band presence alone never confirms a covert device — satphones, legitimate fleet telemetry, and (increasingly) ordinary iPhone 14+ smartphones with satellite SOS all emit in these same L-band uplinks. Confirmation is physical, not spectral.


## cordless_phone_and_intercom_base_stations

Cordless telephone / wireless-headset / intercom / nurse-call base-and-handset RF pairs OUTSIDE the DECT (1.92-1.93 GHz) band. These are legitimate consumer/commercial devices, but a covert audio leak (a live handset left off-hook, a modified base, or a "baby-monitor"-style repurposed cordless set) presents on these same signatures, so a TSCM sweep must cover every legacy cordless allocation, not just DECT. The 900 MHz and 2.4 GHz signatures are fully in stock-Pluto reach. **How to confirm:**
- Demodulate the suspect carrier as narrowband FM: legacy 43-50 MHz and 900/2.4 GHz analog cordless yield intelligible (or voice-inverted) speech — clear audio confirms an analog voice link.

The 900 MHz and 2.4 GHz signatures are fully in stock-Pluto reach. Flag to the client that any surviving analog cordless phone is an open-audio liability independent of whether a bug is found.


## Non-camera Wi-Fi gadget bugs (audio-only Wi-Fi bugs, spy-pen / USB-stick Wi-Fi recorders, smart-plug / charger / adapter-disguised Wi-Fi audio recorders)

This class covers covert devices that carry a microphone (no camera) and exfiltrate audio over 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi 802.11b/g/n — live-listen streams and/or store-and-upload recorders. Physically they hide in pens, USB flash drives, USB wall chargers, mains adapters, extension cords, power banks, and fake "smart plugs." CRITICAL for a TSCM matcher: at the RF/PHY layer these are ORDINARY Wi-Fi devices. An SDR sees 802.11 OFDM/DSSS bursts on standard 2.4 GHz channels — the EXACT same air signature as a Wi-Fi spy cam, a real smart plug, a phone, or a laptop. You CANNOT distinguish an audio bug from a camera or a legitimate IoT device by band/bandwidth/modulation alone. The only physical-layer tells are behavioral: (1) TRAFFIC VOLUME — voice is ~16-256 kbps (often 8-128 kbps ADPCM/AAC/Opus), roughly 100x-1000x lower than the 1-6 Mbps a streaming camera pushes, so the device's uplink airtime/duty is low and bursty rather than a fat continuous stream; (2) DUTY PATTERN — voice-activated (VOX) recorders are silent for long stretches then burst when triggered, and store-and-forward units may go RF-silent for hours then dump a batch; (3) SETUP-MODE BEACON — nearly all briefly run a SoftAP (own SSID/beacon at ~100 ms interval) during provisioning, and factory SSIDs are a strong fingerprint. Because the discriminator is packet-rate and MAC-layer metadata, a stock Pluto/B210 doing raw IQ energy detection can locate and confirm 'a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi emitter is here' but CANNOT by itself say audio-vs-video-vs-legit — that requires a Wi-Fi NIC in monitor mode (Kismet), a per-client airtime/throughput view, or de-authing suspects and watching which device's traffic profile matches voice. All 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi channels (2412-2472 MHz, ch 1-13, plus ch14 2484 in JP) fall inside the stock Pluto AD9364 325-3800 MHz range and inside B210 range, so tuning reach is fine; the limitation is discrimination AND instantaneous capture bandwidth (a single 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel already sits at the Pluto's practical ~20 MHz RX ceiling, and 40 MHz HT40 exceeds it), not center-frequency coverage.

**How to confirm:**
- Do NOT try to ID audio-vs-camera from the SDR spectrum — they are identical 802.11 signatures. Instead put a Wi-Fi NIC in monitor mode (Kismet / airodump-ng) and enumerate every 2.4 GHz client + AP: capture BSSID/MAC OUI, SSID, associated channel, and especially per-client AIRTIME/THROUGHPUT.
- Sort clients by uplink throughput. A camera sustains Mbps; an audio bug trickles tens-to-low-hundreds of kbps in short bursts. Flag any low-rate, always-present client that maps to no known device (phone/laptop/TV/thermostat).
- Cross-check MAC OUI against the device vendor. ESP32/Espressif OUIs, generic module vendors, or an OUI that matches no furniture in the room are suspicious. Note: many bugs randomize/spoof MAC — absence of a hit is not clearance.
- Force SoftAP mode: power-cycle or factory-reset a suspect gadget (unplug the mystery 'charger'/'plug'/pen and re-power) and watch for a new device-hosted SSID beaconing (~100 ms interval, factory-default or gibberish SSID, 192.168.4.1 config page). That AP appearing from an object with no business hosting a network is near-confirmation.
- Physical + power correlation: audio bugs need continuous power in the target room. Inventory every always-powered object — USB chargers, wall warts, 'smart' plugs, power strips, pens left on a desk. Open/weigh/X-ray suspects; a real 5V charger has no microSD slot, no extra PCB, no mic port.
- VOX test: introduce controlled sound (talk / play audio) in the room and watch monitor-mode capture for a client that begins/increases transmitting in time with the sound. A voice-activated bug will key up on audio — a legit smart plug will not.
- De-auth isolation (where lawful/authorized): send a targeted deauth to a suspect client and confirm the object drops offline / re-enters SoftAP, physically tying the MAC to the object.
- Rule out the cellular-fallback case: if the room 'feels bugged' but Wi-Fi is clean, sweep LTE uplink bands with the SDR and near-field probe suspect objects — a SIM-equipped recorder leaves NO 2.4 GHz trace while on 4G.
- Use the SDR (Pluto/B210) for what it's good at here: near-field DF / proximity — walk the antenna to localize which object radiates the 2.4 GHz energy, then hand off to the Wi-Fi NIC + physical search for ID.

**Might legitimately be:**
- Legitimate Wi-Fi security cameras / video doorbells / baby monitors — SAME 2.4 GHz 802.11 signature; only throughput separates them (higher for video). SDR cannot tell them apart from an audio bug.
- Real smart plugs, smart bulbs, smart switches (TP-Link Kasa, Wemo, Tuya, Shelly, Sonoff) — these are LOW-throughput 2.4 GHz clients too, exactly like an audio bug's traffic profile. High false-positive risk: a bug disguised as a smart plug looks identical to a real smart plug on the air.
- Voice assistants (Echo, Google Nest, Sonos), smart thermostats, smart locks, IoT sensors — low-rate 2.4 GHz clients that beacon/poll intermittently, mimicking a VOX bug's bursty pattern.
- Phones, laptops, tablets, smart TVs, printers — ubiquitous 2.4 GHz OFDM emitters that dominate the band and bury a low-rate bug in the noise floor of normal traffic.
- BLE: fitness trackers, earbuds, tags (AirTag/Tile), keyboards/mice — the GFSK advertising signature is indistinguishable from a bug's BLE provisioning beacon.
- Neighbor / building Wi-Fi bleeding through walls — 2.4 GHz clients you can hear but that are not in the target space; wastes time if not spatially gated.
- Microwave ovens, 2.4 GHz cordless phones, Zigbee/Thread hubs, wireless mouse dongles — non-Wi-Fi 2.4 GHz emitters that raise the band's energy floor and can trip a naive IQ energy detector.
- Cellular fallback INVERSE case: a SIM-equipped bug produces NO 2.4 GHz signal at all, so a clean Wi-Fi sweep is a false NEGATIVE, not a clearance.

**TSCM note:** Bottom line for the matcher UI: for this class, the SDR match ('2.4 GHz 802.11 emitter present') is necessary but NOT sufficient — never let the app report 'audio bug found' from spectrum alone. The RF layer of an audio-only Wi-Fi bug is byte-for-byte the same as a Wi-Fi camera and the same as a legitimate smart plug/IoT device. Discrimination lives one layer up (MAC-layer metadata + per-client throughput + duty behavior), which needs a Wi-Fi NIC in monitor mode, not the Pluto. Also be honest about the Pluto's instantaneous bandwidth: a single 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel already sits at the Pluto's practical ~20 MHz RX ceiling and 40 MHz HT40 exceeds it, so the Pluto is a locate/energy-detect tool here, not a channel-capture/demod tool — a B210 (~56 MHz) captures a full channel but still cannot decode 802.11 without a proper NIC/stack. Frame the SDR's job honestly: (a) confirm/locate 2.4 GHz energy near a suspect object (near-field DF), (b) catch the SoftAP setup beacon, (c) sweep LTE uplinks for the cellular-fallback evasion case. Do the actual audio-vs-video-vs-legit call with Kismet/airodump throughput analysis, MAC/OUI enumeration, SoftAP forcing, VOX stimulus testing, and physical inspection of every always-powered object. The three highest-yield moves in practice: (1) inventory and open every powered gadget in the room — these bugs LIVE in chargers/plugs/pens/power strips because they need mains/USB power; (2) force SoftAP by power-cycling suspects and read the beaconed SSID; (3) run a VOX stimulus test and watch which client keys up. Assume MAC randomization/spoofing so a clean OUI list is not clearance, and remember a SIM-equipped unit gives a false-negative on any Wi-Fi-only sweep.


## Bluetooth Classic (BR/EDR) + Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) audio eavesdropping devices: covert earpieces, disguised earbuds/headphones with hidden recorders, BLE/BT body-worn mics, and LE Audio / Auracast broadcast bugs operating in the 2.4 GHz ISM band

This class covers any audio-surveillance device whose over-the-air footprint is a Bluetooth radio: covert in-ear earpieces, "normal-looking" earbuds/headphones with a concealed recorder or live mic, BT/BLE lav mics, and newer LE Audio / Auracast broadcast bugs. On the air they are NOT one signature. Bluetooth splits into two distinct radios - Classic BR/EDR and LE - and each has multiple sub-signatures that differ in bandwidth, modulation, channel plan, and duty. Every signature lives entirely inside 2400-2483.5 MHz. REACH NOTE: 2.4 GHz is WITHIN a stock ADALM-Pluto's 325-3800 MHz tuning range, so all rows here are reachable by stock Pluto AND B210 - the caveat for this band is the opposite of the low-band problem: at 61.44 MSPS max a B210 (56 MHz usable) or Pluto (~20 MHz stable, up to ~56 MHz hacked, USB-throughput dependent) CANNOT see the whole 83.5 MHz ISM band in one shot, so FHSS hoppers must be caught by fast sweeping or energy-detection, not a single wideband capture. Classic BT hops 1600 times/sec across 79 channels (1 MHz raster) and BLE hops across 37 data channels (2 MHz raster); you will almost never decode them with a Pluto/B210 - you detect the ENERGY SIGNATURE (hop pattern, channel raster, burst timing), not the payload. The honest TSCM reality: 2.4 GHz is the single most crowded band on earth (Wi-Fi, every BT device, Zigbee/Thread, microwave ovens, wireless mice/keyboards), so false-positive overlap is extreme and near-total. A BT audio signature alone is almost never actionable - it must be correlated with proximity, timing (only transmits when the target is present/talking), and physical inspection. This is a detection/anomaly-hunting layer, not a clean fingerprint layer.

RF fundamentals to keep straight: Classic uses a 1 MHz channel raster (79 ch) and BLE a 2 MHz raster (3 adv + 37 data); EDR 2/3 Mbps keep the SAME 1 MHz occupied bandwidth as BR (rate comes from bits/symbol, not more spectrum), so only LE2M/LE Audio bursts show the visibly wider ~2 MHz footprint. Modern watch-items: LE Audio 2M-PHY bugs (~2 MHz-wide bursts) and Auracast BROADCAST bugs (connectionless, no pairing handshake, near-continuous ISO cadence) - Auracast in particular defeats old 'detect the pairing' logic and should be an explicit hunt step. Also relevant: 2025 Airoha-chipset research (CVE-2025-20700/20701/20702, ~29 devices across Bose, Sony, Marshall, Jabra, JBL, JLab, Beyerdynamic, Teufel, EarisMax, MoerLabs) showed mainstream earbuds/headsets can be turned into eavesdropping devices by a nearby attacker via BT link-key extraction + HFP mic-listen, requiring only ~10 m proximity - so a benign-looking brand-name earbud left in a sensitive room is a legitimate threat vector, not just purpose-built spy gear. Legal/handling note for the client report: identifying a specific person's device by MAC/name during a sweep can raise privacy issues - keep discovered identifiers internal and report findings as 'unexplained emitter localized to X location,' not as named individuals, unless counsel directs otherwise.


## wireless_mbus_smart_utility_meter

Wireless M-Bus (wM-Bus, EN 13757-4 / OMS) is the dominant European smart-utility telemetry link for gas, water, heat, and electricity meters. From a TSCM standpoint it matters because it is a legitimate, ubiquitous, low-duty-cycle transmitter that (a) is easily mistaken for a covert device, and (b) can itself carry piggybacked exfil data inside its variable-length application payload. It has several genuinely distinct on-air signatures that the generic 863-870 MHz FSK row does not capture: S-mode uses Manchester-coded 2-FSK; T-mode uses a distinctive 3-of-6 chip-coded 2-FSK at 100 kcps; C-mode uses plain NRZ 2-GFSK sharing the same 868.95 MHz center; and N-mode is a completely separate NARROWBAND service at 169 MHz (12.5 kHz channels, 2-GFSK and 4-GFSK) that is OUT of a stock Pluto's 325-3800 MHz tuning range. Because meters transmit in short intermittent bursts (seconds to tens of minutes apart), a sweep must dwell/log over time on these centers rather than rely on a single snapshot. The key TSCM discriminator: a real meter's traffic correlates with a physical utility meter on-site and repeats on a regular telemetry cadence; a device that mimics wM-Bus framing but has no corresponding meter, or transmits far more often/with larger payloads than metering warrants, is suspect.

Both are caught the same way: decode the frames, verify the manufacturer ID + serial resolve to a real on-site meter, physically locate each emitter, and flag anything whose cadence or payload size exceeds what metering needs. Note the reach limits explicitly in any report: 169 MHz N-mode is below a stock Pluto's 325 MHz floor (needs the AD9364 mod or a B210); all 868 MHz modes and the 433 MHz variant are within stock Pluto's 325-3800 MHz range; none of these approach the 6 GHz B210 ceiling.

Practically, decoding (gr-gsm, srsRAN cell search) matters more than raw sweeping here — a phone-based IMSI-catcher-detector app or a dedicated CSS-detection tool complements the SDR. Finally, a confirmed unauthorized cell-site simulator is a serious legal matter (federal spectrum + interception law); the TSCM role is to detect, preserve evidence, and refer to counsel/authorities, not to interact with or spoof the device.

## optical_ir_covert_nonrf

Optical/IR covert devices that emit little or NO intentional RF: IR-illuminated night-vision pinhole cameras, laser/optical listening devices, and purely wired SD-card / internal-memory spy cams. HONEST FLAG: this is the SDR-BLIND class. A Pluto or B210 sweeping the RF spectrum will NOT find these devices, because their signal path is light (visible/IR/laser) or local wire-to-flash storage, not a radio transmitter. There is no legitimate transmit signature to match. They are found by OPTICAL and PHYSICAL means — lens/retro-reflection detectors, IR-viewing cameras that see 850/940 nm glow, physical search, and (for powered electronics regardless of on/off state) a non-linear junction detector (NLJD), which is an active RF-probe instrument, not a passive spectrum sweep. ## ant_ antplus_24ghz_telemetry

ANT / ANT+ is a 2.4 GHz GFSK sport/telemetry protocol (Nordic Enhanced ShockBurst physical layer) that occupies narrow ~1 MHz channels with 1 Mbps GFSK / 160 kHz deviation, arranged 1 MHz apart across 2400-2483.5 MHz. Its distinguishing on-air trait is adaptive-isochronous TDMA: each channel wakes on a fixed period (e.g. ANT+ heart-rate at ~4.06 Hz, other profiles 4-8 Hz) and emits a very short single-packet burst in an assigned time slot, then goes silent. This periodic, time-sliced burst cadence is what separates it from BLE (2 MHz channels, adv-triplet-then-connection-hop) and from Wi-Fi (wideband OFDM). ANT+ concentrates on the 2457 MHz default channel (and 2450 MHz), so a persistent ~1 MHz GFSK carrier ticking at a metronomic sub-10 Hz rate on 2457 MHz is the classic tell. Fully within a stock ADALM-Pluto's 325-3800 MHz tuning range and well inside B210 range; no front-end mod needed. Main practical challenge is not reach but resolving a 1 MHz GFSK burst against dense 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi/BLE/Zigbee traffic and confirming the periodic isochronous cadence rather than a one-off Wi-Fi/BLE hit.

- Bluetooth Classic, which also uses 1 MHz-wide 1 Mbps GFSK channels - distinguished only by its 1600 hops/sec FHSS versus ANT's stationary carrier.
- BLE advertising bursts, which are periodic like ANT but use 2 MHz channels and an advertise-then-hop pattern rather than a single stationary isochronous channel.
- General 2.4 GHz ISM congestion (Wi-Fi OFDM, Zigbee) that can mask or be mistaken for a 1 MHz GFSK burst on a crowded spectrum display.

**TSCM note:** Treat almost every ANT+ hit as benign by default: 2.4 GHz ANT+ is one of the most common consumer fitness-telemetry protocols on earth, and any gym-goer, cyclist, or runner in the vicinity will light up 2457/2450 MHz. The TSCM value is not in detecting ANT+ generically but in spotting the anomalous case - a stationary ~1 MHz GFSK channel with a metronomic isochronous burst cadence sitting on a non-default frequency, running with no plausible fitness device present, or offloading via burst trains. That profile fits a cheap nRF24-family part repurposed as a covert, low-rate, body-worn telemetry or data-exfil link that deliberately hides inside the legitimate ANT population. Note the false-positive minefield: ANT shares the exact 1 Mbps GFSK / 1 MHz-channel ShockBurst waveform with plain Nordic nRF24L01+ links (drones, RC gear, wireless keyboards/mice, some IoT), and is easily confused with Bluetooth Classic (also 1 MHz/1 Mbps GFSK, but FHSS). Resolve by the stationary-carrier + slow-isochronous-tick signature, not by waveform alone. Fully within a stock Pluto (325-3800 MHz) and B210 range - no AD9364 front-end mod needed - so reach is not the limiting factor; demodulating/decoding to prove it is ANT vs generic ShockBurst is the real work, and stock Pluto/B210 IQ plus rtl_433-style ANT decoders can get most of the way. Do not over-claim a covert device from a spectrum hit alone; localize and correlate with a visible wearable before flagging to a client.
